The big idea

The Law of Large Numbers

Imagine flipping a fair coin just 10 times. You might get 7 heads and 3 tails. That's not particularly surprising! Randomness can produce lopsided results in small samples, even when the coin is perfectly fair.

Now imagine flipping that same coin 10,000 times. You probably won't get exactly 5,000 heads, but your proportion of heads will typically be much closer to 50%. As the number of independent flips increases, the experimental proportion tends to settle near the true probability of 0.5.

Experimental probability = Number of successes ÷ Number of trials

In symbols, if \(X_n\) is the number of heads in \(n\) flips, the Law of Large Numbers tells us that

Xn / n → 0.5

as the number of flips \(n\) grows, with probability 1 for an infinite sequence of independent fair-coin flips.

Here's the key: The law does not promise that heads and tails will alternate, that the next flip will be predictable, or that the counts must be exactly equal. It says that the relative frequency of heads tends to approach 50% over many trials.

And there's a subtle twist: the absolute difference between the number of heads and tails can grow even while the percentage difference shrinks. The law is about proportions, not perfect balance!

A fair coin is modeled with independent flips and P(Heads) = P(Tails) = 0.5.
