LEGO Store Analogy

1. The population distribution

Imagine we're at a LEGO store with a massive inventory of bricks in colors spanning the entire visible spectrum.Our store has an enormous inventory. Imagine assigning each brick a numerical value corresponding to its position along the color spectrum.

The population distribution on the left shows how these values are distributed throughout the inventory. Each bar represents a range of values, and its height tells us how many observations fall within that range.

2. Sampling and the sample mean

Now let's reach into the inventory and select n bricks. This is out sample size. We calculate the average numerical value of those bricks. That is, sum the numerical values for all n bricks and divide by the samples size. This average is called the sample mean. Think of it as an average.

No, let's put the bricks back, select another sample of n bricks, and calculate another mean. Repeating this process gives us many sample means.

3. The sampling distribution of the mean

The histogram on the right groups these sample means into its own bins. Each bar counts how many sample means fall within a particular interval. The rainbow colors help us see where those means fall along the numerical axis.

A tall bar means that many samples produced means in that interval. A short bar means fewer did. The number of histogram bins controls the resolution of our view; it does not determine the sample size.

4. What happens as n increases?

When we average only a few bricks, the sample mean can vary considerably from sample to sample. When we average many bricks, unusually high and low values tend to balance each other out. The sample means therefore tend to cluster more tightly around the population mean.

Under the usual conditions of the Central Limit Theorem, the sampling distribution becomes increasingly well approximated by a normal distribution as n grows.

The theoretical standard error
σ / √n

As n increases, the standard error decreases. The theoretical normal curve becomes narrower and taller, while the simulated sample means tend to concentrate near the population mean.

The big idea: We start with individual observations in a population, repeatedly take samples, and study the distribution of their means. The Central Limit Theorem explains why those means tend to form an approximately normal distribution as the sample size grows.
